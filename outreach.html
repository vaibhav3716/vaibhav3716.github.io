Facts from the cosmos

Some of my favourite facts about stars, what they leave behind, and the Universe around them. Tap a card to find out more.

White dwarfs

5 tonnes

The weight of one teaspoon of white dwarf

A white dwarf packs about the Sun's mass into a ball the size of Earth, so its matter weighs roughly a tonne per cubic centimetre. Only the pressure of tightly packed electrons stops it collapsing further.

Source: Worked out from a typical white dwarf density

White dwarfs

97%

Of all stars will end their lives as white dwarfs

Every star born with less than about 8 to 10 times the Sun's mass ends as a white dwarf. That includes the Sun, in roughly 5 billion years.

Source: Fontaine, Brassard & Bergeron 2001, PASP 113, 409

White dwarfs

1.4 M☉

The heaviest a white dwarf can ever be

Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar worked out this limit in 1930, aged 19, on the voyage from India to England. Heavier remnants collapse into neutron stars or black holes. It earned him a share of the 1983 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Source: The Nobel Prize in Physics 1983

White dwarfs

Frozen

Old white dwarfs turn solid as they cool

Gaia's measurements of thousands of nearby white dwarfs show a pile-up where their carbon–oxygen cores crystallise. The heat released as they freeze slows their cooling by up to 2 billion years.

Source: Tremblay et al. 2019, Nature 565, 202

White dwarfs

Earth-sized

Sirius B, the hidden partner of the brightest star in the sky

Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, has a white dwarf companion with about the mass of the Sun (1.02 M☉) squeezed into a sphere slightly smaller than Earth.

Source: Bond et al. 2017, ApJ 840, 70

Binary stars

70%+

Of massive stars will swap mass with a partner

Most massive O-type stars have a close companion, and more than 70% will exchange mass with it during their lives. That reshapes how they evolve, and how they explode.

Source: Sana et al. 2012, Science 337, 444

Binary stars

2.87 days

How often Algol, the ‘Demon Star’, fades

Algol is an eclipsing binary: every 2.87 days the fainter star passes in front of the brighter one. Oddly, its less massive star is the more evolved one, because it has already handed much of its mass to its partner.

Source: The Algol paradox, a classic case of mass transfer

The Sun and stars

8 min 20 s

Sunlight's journey to Earth

Light leaving the Sun's surface reaches us in about 8 minutes 20 seconds. But the energy it carries spent somewhere between 10,000 and 170,000 years scattering its way out from the core.

Source: NASA

The Sun and stars

4 million tonnes

The mass the Sun turns into light every second

The Sun fuses about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium each second. The 4 million tonnes that go missing leave as energy, exactly as E = mc2 says.

Source: Worked out from the Sun's luminosity, 3.83 × 1026 W

The Sun and stars

Past Mars

Where Betelgeuse would reach if it replaced the Sun

The red supergiant on Orion's shoulder is roughly 700 times wider than the Sun. Put in the Sun's place, its surface would stretch beyond the orbit of Mars.

Source: ESO and NASA

Neutron stars and black holes

716 turns/s

The fastest-spinning neutron star known

The pulsar PSR J1748−2446ad, in the globular cluster Terzan 5, spins 716 times every second. A point on its equator moves at tens of thousands of kilometres per second.

Source: Hessels et al. 2006, Science 311, 1901

Neutron stars and black holes

Billions of tonnes

The weight of one teaspoon of neutron star

A neutron star crushes more than the Sun's mass into a ball about 20 km across. A teaspoon of it would weigh a few billion tonnes, several times the combined mass of every person alive.

Source: Worked out from a typical neutron star density

Neutron stars and black holes

3 M☉

Turned into gravitational waves in a split second

On 14 September 2015 LIGO caught two black holes, of 36 and 29 solar masses, merging 1.3 billion light-years away. About three Suns' worth of mass left as ripples in spacetime in a fraction of a second.

Source: LIGO and Virgo, Abbott et al. 2016, PRL 116, 061102

Neutron stars and black holes

6.5 billion M☉

The first black hole ever photographed

In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope, an Earth-sized network of radio dishes, imaged the shadow of the black hole at the heart of the galaxy M87, 55 million light-years away.

Source: Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration 2019, ApJL 875, L1

The Universe

1912

The year a pulsing star became a cosmic ruler

Henrietta Swan Leavitt found that brighter Cepheid variables pulse more slowly. That let Edwin Hubble measure the distance to Andromeda in the 1920s and show it is a galaxy far beyond our own.

Source: Leavitt & Pickering 1912, Harvard College Observatory Circular 173

The Universe

2.725 K

The temperature of the Big Bang’s afterglow

The cosmic microwave background is light released about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when the Universe first became transparent. Today it fills all of space at just 2.725 degrees above absolute zero.

Source: Fixsen 2009, ApJ 707, 916

The Universe

75,000 years

Voyager 1's travel time to the nearest star

Proxima Centauri is 4.24 light-years away. At its 17 km/s, Voyager 1, one of the fastest spacecraft ever launched, would need about 75,000 years to cover that distance.

Source: NASA/JPL

The Universe

243 days

A day on Venus lasts longer than its year

Venus turns once every 243 Earth days, and backwards, but goes round the Sun in 225. So a Venusian day is longer than a Venusian year.

Source: NASA

White dwarfs

27%

Of young white dwarfs are swallowing the remains of planets

Hubble found metals in the atmospheres of more than half the young white dwarfs it studied, and 27% must be taking in rocky material right now. The likeliest source is asteroids or planets torn apart by the white dwarf's gravity.

Source: Koester et al. 2014, A&A 566, A34

White dwarfs

1.4 days

How fast a giant planet circles a dead star

WD 1856+534 b, found by NASA's TESS, is a Jupiter-sized planet orbiting a white dwarf every 1.4 days. The star is only about as big as Earth, so the planet is roughly seven times wider than the star it circles.

Source: Vanderburg et al. 2020, Nature 585, 363

White dwarfs

0

Black dwarfs anywhere in the Universe

A white dwarf slowly cools and fades. Cooling all the way to a cold, dark ‘black dwarf’ takes far longer than the Universe's 13.8-billion-year age, so not a single one has formed yet.

Source: White dwarf cooling theory; the Universe's age from Planck Collaboration 2020, A&A 641, A6

White dwarfs

2011

The Nobel Prize won with exploding white dwarfs

Type Ia supernovae, white dwarfs in binaries blowing themselves apart, are so evenly bright that they work as distance markers. In 1998 they showed that the Universe's expansion is speeding up, which earned the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Source: The Nobel Prize in Physics 2011

White dwarfs

100,000×

Gravity on a white dwarf's surface, compared with Earth

At the surface of a typical white dwarf, gravity is about 100,000 times Earth's. A 70 kg person standing there would weigh around 7,000 tonnes.

Source: Worked out from a typical white dwarf surface gravity (log g ≈ 8)

Binary stars

Years to hours

How a shared envelope shrinks a binary star's orbit

When a giant star swells and engulfs its companion, both orbit inside one shared envelope of gas. Drag pulls them together until the envelope is thrown off, leaving a white dwarf and its partner circling in hours instead of years. My own research hunts for pairs like these.

Source: Ivanova et al. 2013, A&A Review 21, 59

Binary stars

5.4 minutes

How often the white dwarfs of HM Cancri circle each other

HM Cancri is a pair of white dwarfs orbiting each other every 5.4 minutes, one of the tightest binaries known. They are slowly spiralling together as they give off gravitational waves.

Source: Roelofs et al. 2010, ApJL 711, L138

Binary stars

46%

Of Sun-like stars have at least one companion

A careful census of 454 nearby Sun-like stars found that 46% have a companion star or brown dwarf. The other 54% are single, like the Sun.

Source: Raghavan et al. 2010, ApJS 190, 1

Binary stars

Two suns

Kepler-16b, a real planet with double sunsets

Kepler-16b is a Saturn-sized planet that orbits a pair of stars every 229 days, so it would see two suns set, like Tatooine in Star Wars. It was the first planet found passing in front of two stars.

Source: Doyle et al. 2011, Science 333, 1602

Neutron stars and black holes

21 M☉

Cygnus X-1, the first black hole ever identified

Cygnus X-1 was found as a bright X-ray source in 1964 and recognised as a black hole in the early 1970s. It weighs about 21 Suns and pulls gas off a giant blue companion star, 7,200 light-years away.

Source: Miller-Jones et al. 2021, Science 371, 1046

Neutron stars and black holes

450 per second

How fast the X-rays near a black hole can flicker

In black hole X-ray binaries the X-rays flicker in near-regular rhythms called quasi-periodic oscillations, some as fast as 450 times a second. They come from gas swirling very close to the black hole, which makes them a probe of gravity at its strongest.

Source: Remillard & McClintock 2006, ARA&A 44, 49

Neutron stars and black holes

2.35 M☉

The heaviest well-measured neutron star

PSR J0952−0607 packs about 2.35 Suns into a ball the size of a city and spins 707 times a second. It is a ‘black widow’ pulsar, slowly evaporating the small companion star that fed it.

Source: Romani et al. 2022, ApJL 934, L17

Neutron stars and black holes

2017

The year colliding neutron stars were both heard and seen

On 17 August 2017 LIGO and Virgo caught gravitational waves from two neutron stars merging 130 million light-years away, and telescopes worldwide then watched the explosion. Its glow carried the signature of freshly made heavy elements, the family that includes gold and platinum.

Source: LIGO and Virgo, Abbott et al. 2017, PRL 119, 161101

Neutron stars and black holes

4 million M☉

The black hole at the centre of our galaxy

Sagittarius A*, 26,000 light-years away, has about 4 million times the Sun's mass. Decades of tracking stars racing around it proved it is a black hole, and earned Reinhard Genzel and Andrea Ghez a share of the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Source: The Nobel Prize in Physics 2020

Neutron stars and black holes

1015 gauss

The magnetic field of a magnetar

Magnetars are neutron stars with the strongest magnetic fields known, up to about 1015 gauss, some thousand trillion times Earth's. The field is so strong that it cracks the star's crust, setting off bursts of X-rays and gamma rays.

Source: Kaspi & Beloborodov 2017, ARA&A 55, 261

Neutron stars and black holes

1967

The year a PhD student found the first pulsar

Jocelyn Bell Burnell, a PhD student at Cambridge, spotted a radio signal repeating every 1.34 seconds. Jokingly labelled LGM-1, for ‘little green men’, it turned out to be a spinning neutron star.

Source: Hewish et al. 1968, Nature 217, 709

The Sun and stars

75%

Of the stars near the Sun are red dwarfs

Small, cool red dwarfs make up 284 of the 378 stars within 33 light-years of us. They are so dim that almost none of them can be seen without a telescope.

Source: RECONS census of stars within 10 parsecs

The Sun and stars

15 million °C

The temperature at the Sun's core

The Sun's core is about 15 million °C, hot and dense enough to fuse hydrogen into helium. Its visible surface, by comparison, is a mild 5,500 °C.

Source: NASA

The Sun and stars

65 billion

Solar neutrinos passing through your thumbnail every second

The Sun's core also releases ghostly particles called neutrinos. About 65 billion of them pass through every square centimetre of you each second, day and night, because they fly straight through the Earth.

Source: The standard solar model

The Universe

2.5 million years

The age of the light you see from the Andromeda Galaxy

Andromeda is about 2.5 million light-years away, so the light reaching you tonight left it around the time the first members of our genus, Homo, appeared on Earth. It is the most distant thing most people can see with the naked eye.

Source: NASA

The Universe

85%

Of the matter in the Universe is dark matter

Everything we can see, from stars and gas to planets and people, makes up only about 15% of the Universe's matter. The rest is dark matter, which gives off no light and is known only through its gravity.

Source: Planck Collaboration 2020, A&A 641, A6

The Universe

90%

Of your body's mass was made inside stars

The oxygen, carbon, nitrogen and calcium in your body were forged inside stars and scattered when they died. Only the hydrogen, about a tenth of your weight, dates back to the Big Bang.

Source: Worked out from the body's make-up by mass
